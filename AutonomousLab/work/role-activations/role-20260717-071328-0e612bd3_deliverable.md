## General-reader level

Spacetime around a point looks like "one time direction and three space
directions" - the `1+3` split. A tempting idea in the null-edge program: the
finite operator that carries mass is built from four "layers" of neighbours
(events zero, one, two, and three steps into the past). Four layers, four
spacetime directions - maybe the layers ARE the directions, with one of them
playing the role of time?

The kernel says no, and says exactly why. Each layer comes with a sign fixed by
the program's own mass coefficients `(1, -9, 16, -8)`: after the built-in sign
flip the four layers carry signs `(-, +, -, +)`. To be a `1+3` spacetime split
you need ONE direction of one sign and THREE of the other (that is what
"one time, three space" means as a signature). But `(-, +, -, +)` is two-and-two
- perfectly balanced, never one-and-three, no matter how many neighbours sit in
each layer. So the four mass-layers, by themselves, cannot be the four
directions of spacetime. [KERNEL]

Reading (interpretation, not a theorem): this is a useful failure. It tells the
program that the "time" direction and the "three space" directions must come
from somewhere richer than just counting how many steps into the past a
neighbour sits - they have to come from the actual spatial arrangement of the
neighbours. Not claimed: that no construction can build a frame - only that
THIS particular shortcut cannot.

"More layers / more modes = more structure = closer to a spacetime frame." The
`(2,2)` result is the counterexample: the four layers are maximally balanced,

talk has the wrong picture; this piece replaces it with "the signs are fixed
and balanced, so the frame must come from spatial arrangement, not layer
